About ReCursor

Photographs have traditionally been relied upon to record the world. They were produced to be the keepers of happy memories, the sources for news, a way to see with your own eyes that what was said or written was true. Witnessing, proving, illustrating, capturing elements of and events in the world out there are all still things photographs can do. But in the last couple of decades, through digital circulation, they arguably increasingly create our world.

Photographs serve as vernacular languages on the internet, with the visual literacy of print becoming replaced by a new visual fluency as the distinction between makers and viewers was eroded. By sharing images across digital networks, we all participate in transforming what photographs can do. More explicitly and fluently than before, still images function as dynamic vessels of meaning, existing enmeshed within planetary-scale databases and travelling endlessly throughout communications networks. An image made on a mobile phone in Chicago, say, and shared to a social media feed might strike a chord for a German viewer who, by reposting it, propels it onward to reemerge as a meme in a Japanese pop culture page, or in a dataset used to train facial recognition systems in San Francisco, or transforming an interior designer's moodboard in Sao Paolo.

In 2023 the MA Photography and Society (MAPS) initiated the Networked Audience research group, which set out to explore questions of how and where photographs travel online, and how these journeys transform them, their messages, their relationship to other types of imagery including AI generated ones, and the people they encounter. In our work we explored recirculation of colonial archives, WhatsApp as a publishing channel, recursivity as a creative force, and interfaces' influence on informational structures. We also organised two public symposia, at Rotterdam's Nederlands Fotomuseum and later the Kunstmuseum in The Hague, at which international speakers presented papers and artworks reflecting on the transformation of the medium and its audiences.

This special edition of Trigger magazine continues that discussion and expands it into new and intriguing directions. From an exploration of the Digital Treasury, which reconstructs and makes accessible Ireland's national archives that were destroyed in 1922, to reflections on nostalgia and temporality inspired by a trio of Instagram pages created to memorialise the post-Soviet era in Latvia, Lithuania and Estonia, this publication addresses the ongoing transformation of photography from the perspective of the photographic audience. Along the way, each contributor formulates their own answer to the question of what it means to audiences when photography is understood not as a means of inscription but a medium of exchange.

The publication exists as a bot to be accessed mainly through the Telegram app (though it will also open in a desktop browser). Telegram was chosen as a platform because it enables direct-to-reader publishing and allows reader response. We recognise that it is a politically problematic platform, but we believe that to reach new audiences we must go where those audiences are. As with much of the digital world, our use of the platform does not imply any support for its owners.
